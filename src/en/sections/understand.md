# Factors influencing the environmental impact of generative AI

The main environmental impacts related to the use of AI that we will examine in this article are the following:

- **Greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions** related to the electricity and other energy sources needed to run the data centers that power AI models;
- **Water consumption** to cool the IT equipment in data centers.

Other impacts could be considered, but since the subject is still somewhat immature, we will limit ourselves to these two.

## The fundamentals: the Large Language Model (LLM)

In a chatbot like ChatGPT, a mechanism generates responses that give the impression of a real conversation. This is the LLM: Large Language Model. It is a generative artificial intelligence model, trained on a very large amount of text in order to learn the characteristics of, and relationships between, words and sentences. Its main goal is to predict, based on the context provided, which words are most likely to come next.

## Tokens

LLMs do not process words directly, but **tokens**. An *embedder* (a transformation tool) breaks words down into tokens before processing. A useful rule of thumb is to consider that a token generally corresponds to about 4 characters in typical English text. This is equivalent to about three-quarters of a word (so 100 tokens correspond to about 75 words) [@openai2026tokenizer].

**Example of tokenization:**
"Impacting the environment" breaks down into 4 tokens: "Impact / ing / the / environment"

LLM designers use different *embedders*, which means that the same text can produce a different number of tokens depending on the model.

Tokens can be divided into two categories: 
- input tokens (also called: instruction tokens, user tokens, or sent tokens)
- output tokens (also called: generated tokens, assistant tokens, or received tokens).

### The more tokens the AI has to process, the more energy it consumes

The number of output tokens is theoretically proportional to total energy consumption. 

**Example:** An exchange that requires processing **10 tokens** consumes **half as much energy** as a request that processes **20 tokens**.

### Output tokens generally consume more energy than input tokens

Some studies show that processing an exchange with a long sent message (for example, 50,000 tokens) consumes only slightly more energy than short sent messages [@Caravaca2026]. This suggests that the consumption of an input token is negligible compared to an output token.
In reality, its consumption varies greatly depending on the total length of the message. It can be lower when the message is short, but higher when the message is long. The same is true for output tokens [@vartziotis2026tokenswatthoursanalyticalenergy; @373c7a76c4004545bb3bb80df83f2932].

**Simplified model**:
A request with **1,000 input tokens** and **50 output tokens** will theoretically consume **less energy** than a request with **50 input tokens** and **1,000 output tokens**.

Assuming that most exchanges with a chatbot are long, the consumption of an input token cannot be considered negligible [@ruf2026cost]. 

**General rule**: the fewer output tokens a request processes, the less energy it will consume. The same goes for input tokens, but generally to a lesser degree than for output tokens.

### Reasoning tokens add to output tokens

A reasoning model first produces a planning text that it relies on to build its final response.
The tokens generated by the reasoning model are sometimes **not visible** to the user. A reasoning token consumes **as much energy** as an output token. They increase the **total number of tokens processed** by the LLM, and therefore its energy consumption [@felipe2026reasoning].


We therefore understand that the number of tokens processed, particularly output tokens, has a strong impact on the environmental footprint.
To measure this impact and quantify its energy, carbon, and water footprint, three key indicators can be tracked:

- GHG emissions, expressed as mass of carbon dioxide equivalent (gCO₂e);
- energy consumption, expressed in watt-hours (Wh);
- water consumption, expressed as volume (L or m³).

These indicators apply to both input tokens and output tokens.

## The major role of the data center

A generative AI, like an LLM, is a program run on a computer. It can be your personal machine, but it is generally done in data centers. Running it requires energy, which causes an environmental impact, measured here in **gCO₂e/kWh**.

The different services for using an LLM, which consume energy, are as follows:

- **Hosting in a public data center** (Microsoft Azure, Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud Platform): the AI runs on servers in a data center owned by a company (cloud) rather than directly on your computer. These servers are very powerful and make it possible to run AI models that you have downloaded or that you have developed and trained yourself. In this case, you own the AI and choose the cloud provider and the geographic region in which it will run.
- **Hosting in your own data center**: generally reserved for companies, the principle is similar to the public cloud: you own the AI and decide the location of the machine on which to run it.
- **In the data centers of official AI providers** like OpenAI, Anthropic (Claude), Google, Mistral AI, Meta, DeepSeek, Z.ai, Moonshot AI, or aggregators like OpenRouter, Vercel, Groq, and MammouthAI. Here, you control nothing about how the AI runs. It may be hosted in a private data center, or with a public cloud provider (in the name of the provider or the aggregator).
- **On your computer**: some recent, small AIs can run directly on your computer while delivering performance competitive with large proprietary models [@saadfalcon2026intelligencewattmeasuringintelligence]. You then control 100% of the energy consumption related to its use and can use generative AI offline.

### The cost of electricity production by country

Each country produces electricity with a different environmental impact. To generate **1 kWh**, a power plant emits GHGs and consumes water. 

Some organizations provide the carbon emission factors of each country (in gCO₂e/kWh produced):
- the **International Energy Agency** (IEA), normally paid but available free of charge on request [@iea2025emissions];
- the **ADEME**, free but uses 2011 IEA data [@ademe2026];
- Ember Energy, free and uses 2025 data from the IEA, Eurostat, BP, and UN [@ember_yearly_electricity_data];
- Spectrum IEEE, free and by country or type of production [@jones2008water].

According to Ember Energy, these factors can range from just over 20 (Iceland) to more than 800 gCO₂e/kWh (Mongolia). The **location of the servers** plays a major role in the environmental impact of AI.

### Data centers must be cooled

When servers are in use, the electricity they receive is converted into heat.
To keep running properly, the servers in a data center must be kept at an optimal temperature.
To remove the heat, data centers use different cooling methods [@le_goff2023], including:

- **Direct outside air cooling**:
Possible in cool-climate regions, this method ventilates outside air to cool the servers. It consumes electricity mainly to power the fans and does not consume water.
- **Adiabatic cooling**:
Hot outside air is ventilated and cooled by spraying fine water droplets that evaporate on contact with it. This method consumes both electricity and water.
- **Direct Chip Cooling**:
Water flows through micro-pipes between the server components to cool them directly. Although the water is not consumed, it must be cooled at some point in the circuit, which often requires energy. In addition, this water can be drawn on by the data center during periods of high demand (for example, for human consumption or agricultural irrigation in summer), which can create competition over usage.


#### Water consumption can be measured

The **WUE** (*Water Usage Effectiveness*) is an indicator measuring the amount of water consumed by data centers [@green_grid2011_wue].
This so-called consumed water is actually evaporated into the atmosphere. Water withdrawn from the environment and returned later is not accounted for in the calculation of this indicator.

**Formula:** WUE (L/kWh) = Water consumed (L) / Energy consumed by servers (kWh)

WUE depends mainly on the location of the data center and its cooling methods.
Those located in arid regions generally have a higher WUE, and therefore use more water during periods of high heat.

### Energy efficiency index: the PUE

The same server consumes the same amount of energy, **regardless of its location**. 
However, the energy needed to keep it at an optimal temperature varies with the climate. Thus, more or less energy is needed to cool it depending on its location.

The indicator for measuring the energy efficiency of a data center, particularly for cooling and infrastructure maintenance, is the **PUE** (*Power Usage Effectiveness*) [@Malone2006]. It represents the ratio between:

- **the total consumption of the data center**;
- **the consumption related solely to running the servers**.

**Formula:** PUE = Total data center consumption (kWh) / Data center server consumption (kWh)

**Examples of interpretation:**

- **PUE = 1**: ideal. All the energy is used solely to run the servers.
- **PUE > 1**: in addition to the energy to run the servers, the data center also consumes energy to cool the servers.
- **PUE = 2**: as much energy is consumed to power the servers as to cool them.
- **PUE > 2**: very bad. More energy is consumed to cool the servers than to power them.

The global average PUE is 1.54 [@taylor2026], while Google's is 1.09 [@google2026pue].

The data center **PUE** is a key criterion when choosing a cloud provider and a hosting region.

## Choosing the AI model to use

### Model size

The size of a language model is measured by its number of parameters. **The more parameters a model activates during execution, the higher its energy consumption per token**. This consumption is directly proportional to the number of parameters.

**Example:** a model with 100 billion parameters consumes **half as much electricity** to process a token as a model with 200 billion parameters [@mistralai2025environmental].

This rule does not necessarily apply across all model providers. Indeed, each uses different design methods, which affects the energy consumption of the models [@Zschache2026]. However, it can generally be applied to all models within the same family: GPT-6 models among themselves, Gemini 3 models among themselves, etc.
Thus, even if this rule is not universal for comparing all models with one another, it remains a reliable indicator for guiding the choice of a model from the same provider.

**Provider transparency**:
Open-source models display their number of parameters. But the number for models from private providers like OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google is never disclosed. It is nevertheless possible to rank a provider's models by size using their names: *luna, lite, small, nano, mini < terra, flash, medium < sol, pro, large, plus, max*.
Some research attempts to quantify this number of parameters using various innovative methods, such as comparing the volume of stored knowledge [@li2026incompressibleknowledgeprobesestimating].

It is also possible to estimate the size of proprietary models by comparing their price to that of open models. However, this method is highly uncertain because a price decrease can mean an optimization rather than a decrease in the number of parameters [@du2026tieredsupermooreslawprice].

### MoE models only activate the useful parameters

A **MoE** (*Mixture of Experts*) model is a type of model that does **not use all of its parameters** when it runs. This does not make it any less capable; on the contrary, it activates only the parameters needed to generate the response. For example, a MoE model with 300 billion parameters may activate only 30 billion of them, which makes it **much less energy-hungry** than a dense model with 100 billion parameters [@mu2026comprehensivesurveymixtureofexpertsalgorithms].
By activating a **small number of parameters**, MoE models reduce the energy consumption of processing a token.

## Inference engines

An LLM's energy consumption during execution is not determined solely by the model itself: it also depends on the inference engine used [@niu2025inferenceengine].
An inference engine is, in a way, the software that runs an LLM at the moment you ask it a question.
If the LLM were a car engine, the inference engine would be the system that organizes and controls its operation.
Some providers optimize their models for certain inference engines [@nvidia2026llminference].
